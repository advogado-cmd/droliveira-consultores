An accounting audit answers whether the numbers are correct. A due diligence answers another question: what, in this company, holds growth back, and in what order to solve it. That is why it reads at the same time the articles of association, the invoices, the licences, the insurance policy, payroll, clinical documents and the competitors.

## Fourteen fronts, four families

Every company gets the same reading: financial fronts (accounting, liquidity, tax), strategic (market, sales, brand), operational (management, key person, equipment, data) and structural and regulatory (corporate, assets, labour, licences). Each is rated critical, high, medium or low.

## The central knot

There is almost always one fact that produces chain effects. In a recent case, a clinic with 26 years of operation had thirteen fronts rated critical or high, and the origin was a single one: the company registration lacked the activity code for the service that generated 71% of revenue. Once fixed, the rest moved.

## What comes out at the end

An interactive report with document repository, the front matrix, a licence checklist, an on-site visit classified by area and the plan in numbered workstreams: what to do, who does it, what it costs, when. It ends with the decisions management needs to take, usually three.

## And the good news is usually the same

Most of what is wrong can be fixed by documentary acts, not by changing the business. The good company already exists; the paperwork just has to catch up with reality.
